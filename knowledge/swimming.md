# Swimming

## Summary
Swimming is the only endurance discipline where technique, not fitness, is usually the limiter — an aerobically strong athlete can be slow in the water in a way that has no equivalent on the bike or on foot. Water is roughly 800 times denser than air, so drag rises with the square of velocity and the returns on reducing drag dwarf the returns on producing more power. The practical consequence for a fit multisport athlete is counterintuitive: the fastest route to a better swim split is often *less* aerobic work and more technical work, which is the opposite of the advice that works everywhere else. Sheila Taormina's position is the most extreme (propulsion mechanics over drag reduction over fitness); Gerry Rodrigues and the open-water school argue that pool technique optimizes for the wrong environment; Joel Filliol and the elite-coaching tradition argue that at some point you simply have to swim a lot. For an adult-onset swimmer, all three are partly right, and which one applies depends on where the athlete actually is.

## Key Concepts

### Why Swimming Is Different
- **Drag dominates.** Resistance rises with the square of velocity in a medium ~800× denser than air. A small reduction in frontal area or a fixed body-position fault returns more speed than a large increase in fitness.
- **Economy varies enormously.** Between two athletes of identical aerobic capacity, swim speed can differ by 40%+ on technique alone. In running the same gap is a few percent.
- **Feel for the water is trainable but slow.** Proprioceptive skill in water develops over years, not weeks. This is why age-group swimmers who started as children retain an advantage that adult-onset athletes rarely close.
- **Aerobic transfer is asymmetric.** Cycling and running fitness transfers into swimming poorly; swimming fitness transfers out somewhat better. Do not assume a strong bike engine will show up in the water.

### Critical Swim Speed (CSS)
The swimming analogue of functional threshold — the pace theoretically sustainable without accumulating fatigue, roughly a 30-minute maximal effort.

**Test:** time a maximal 400 m and a maximal 200 m, well rested, with full recovery between.

```
CSS pace (sec per 100 m) = (400 − 200) / (T400 − T200)
```

Worked example: 400 m in 6:40 (400 s), 200 m in 3:10 (190 s) → (400−200)/(400−190) = 200/210 = **0.95 s/m = 1:35 per 100 m**.

Retest every 8–12 weeks. CSS drifts with technique change far more than with fitness change, which is itself diagnostic: a large CSS improvement without a training-volume increase means the technical work is working.

### Zones by CSS
| Zone | % of CSS pace | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery | CSS + 10–15 s/100 m | Technique focus, active recovery |
| Aerobic / endurance | CSS + 5–10 s/100 m | Base volume, the bulk of the work |
| Threshold | CSS ± 2 s/100 m | Sustained race-effort tolerance |
| VO2max | CSS − 3–6 s/100 m | Short intervals, 50–200 m repeats |
| Speed | maximal | Neuromuscular, 25–50 m |

### Pool vs Open Water
These are close to different sports and training in one does not fully prepare for the other.

| | Pool | Open water |
|---|---|---|
| Walls | Push-off every 25–50 m — free speed and rest | None; continuous effort |
| Line | Painted, no navigation | Sighting costs 2–5 s per 100 m and disrupts rhythm |
| Water | Flat, temperature-controlled | Chop, current, cold, variable |
| Company | Own lane | Contact, drafting, washing-machine starts |
| Stroke rate | Lower, longer | Higher, shorter — chop punishes a long glide |

A pool-optimized stroke — long, glide-heavy, low stroke rate — is often actively slower in chop. This is the core of the Rodrigues critique.

### Drafting
Legal in most open-water and non-draft-legal triathlon swims, and worth 10–25% energy savings. Two positions:
- **Feet / direct draft:** directly behind the lead swimmer's feet. Largest saving, requires constant attention, easy to lose.
- **Hip draft:** beside the lead swimmer's hip. Smaller saving but far easier to hold, and better for sighting.

For an athlete who is not swim-limited by fitness, drafting is the single largest available time saving in an open-water race — larger than a season of technique work.

### Wetsuits
- Buoyancy raises the legs and hips, reducing drag. Typical benefit is **3–7 s per 100 m**, and the benefit is *largest for the weakest swimmers*, because they have the worst body position to begin with.
- Shoulder flexibility is the cost. A suit that restricts the catch can cancel its own benefit.
- Legality is temperature-dependent and governed by the race body. Practise in the suit; do not race in an untested one.

## What the Experts Say

### Sheila Taormina (Swim Speed Secrets; Olympic swimmer and triathlete)
- **Propulsion over everything:** argues the sport over-indexes on drag reduction and body position, and that the actual differentiator among fast swimmers is what the hand and forearm do underwater. "The most important thing is the pull."
- **The high-elbow catch (early vertical forearm):** the forearm is oriented as a paddle facing backward as early in the stroke as possible. This is the single technical element she considers non-negotiable.
- **Stroke rate matters more than distance-per-stroke:** criticizes the glide-heavy, low-stroke-count orthodoxy popular in adult swim instruction. Elite distance swimmers turn the arms over faster than most age-groupers are taught to.
- **Against counting strokes as a goal:** a low stroke count achieved by gliding is dead time, not efficiency.

### Gerry Rodrigues (Tower 26; open-water specialist)
- **Train for the environment you race in.** Pool-optimized technique is a liability in open water. Advocates deliberately higher stroke rate ("swim with a faster tempo than feels natural") to handle chop and to keep momentum without walls.
- **Open-water-specific skills are trainable and neglected:** sighting rhythm (every 4–8 strokes rather than at random), turning around buoys, starts, contact tolerance, and drafting. These return more time for a mid-pack triathlete than aerobic gains.
- **Bilateral breathing as a tool, not a virtue:** breathe to the side away from chop and sun; the ability to breathe both sides matters, the obligation to alternate every three strokes does not.

### Joel Filliol / elite triathlon coaching tradition
- **Frequency beats duration.** Five to six shorter swims per week beats two or three long ones for skill acquisition, because feel for the water decays fast. This is the strongest consensus in elite swim coaching and the hardest for a time-limited age-grouper to act on.
- **Volume is not optional at the top.** For athletes already technically sound, swim speed responds to swim volume the way every other endurance discipline responds to volume. The technique-first argument has a ceiling.
- **The swim is the smallest lever in a long triathlon.** In an Ironman the swim is roughly 10% of total race time; over-investing in it at the expense of the bike is a common and expensive error.

### Jason Koop (CTS)
- Not a swim coach, but his workload framework applies: **specificity of training to the demands of the race.** For a triathlete this cuts against generic swim fitness and toward race-condition rehearsal — wetsuit, open water, mass start, the actual distance.

## Where They Agree
1. **Technique is the limiter for adult-onset swimmers.** All sources agree that for an athlete who did not swim as a child, technical work returns more than aerobic work — at least initially.
2. **The catch is where propulsion comes from.** High-elbow forearm orientation is universal across every source that discusses mechanics.
3. **Frequency beats duration for skill.** Short, frequent swims develop feel better than infrequent long ones.
4. **Open-water skills are separately trainable and usually neglected.** Sighting, drafting, and starts are cheap time.
5. **Video is the highest-return diagnostic.** Swimmers cannot perceive their own body position. Everything else is guesswork by comparison.

## Where They Disagree

### Technique vs Volume
- **Taormina:** propulsion mechanics first, and most swimmers never get them right, so most swimmers should keep working on them.
- **Filliol / elite tradition:** technique-first has a ceiling and athletes stall at it. Past a competent stroke, you have to swim a lot.
- **Practical resolution:** the honest test is whether CSS improves when volume increases. If it does, volume is the lever. If volume rises and CSS is flat, the limiter is technical.

### Stroke Rate vs Distance Per Stroke
- **Taormina and Rodrigues:** higher stroke rate; the low-stroke-count orthodoxy produces dead glide.
- **Total Immersion school:** long, gliding stroke, minimize stroke count.
- **Where the evidence sits:** elite distance swimmers use higher stroke rates than age-groupers are typically taught, and chop punishes glide. The low-count approach is most defensible for a beginner learning body position, and least defensible in open water.

### How Much the Swim Matters
- **Rodrigues:** it sets up the whole race; a bad swim costs more than the clock shows because of the state you exit the water in.
- **Filliol:** in long-course racing it is ~10% of the day and is routinely over-trained relative to the bike.
- **Both are right at different distances.** In a sprint the swim is a large fraction and positioning matters enormously. In an Ironman it is a small fraction and arriving fresh matters more than arriving fast.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 1: Establish where the limiter actually is
- Test CSS (400/200 protocol).
- Get **video** — front, side, and underwater if possible. Body position faults are invisible from inside the stroke.
- Compare swim performance to bike and run performance relative to age-group norms. A large negative outlier in the water means technique, not fitness.

### Step 2: Assign the limiter
| Finding | Limiter | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Legs sink, large gap between CSS and aerobic capacity | Body position | Drag reduction: head position, core, kick timing. Highest return available. |
| Body position fine, weak catch on video | Propulsion | Taormina catch work, paddles/band sparingly, sculling drills |
| Technically sound, CSS improves with volume | Fitness | Add volume and frequency; standard endurance progression |
| Pool times good, race times poor | Open-water skills | Sighting, drafting, wetsuit, mass-start practice |

### Step 3: Set frequency before duration
Three swims a week is the practical floor for maintaining feel; four to five is where skill acquisition happens. Two long swims a week is the worst common pattern — enough fatigue to cost recovery, not enough frequency to build skill.

### Step 4: Build the week
- **Technique**: every session, in the warmup, when fresh. Never drill fatigued — you rehearse the fault.
- **Aerobic**: the bulk, at CSS + 5–10 s/100 m.
- **Threshold**: one session weekly, e.g. 8–10 × 100 m at CSS with 15 s rest.
- **Speed**: short, sparing, 25–50 m efforts. Neuromuscular, not conditioning.
- **Open water**: at least fortnightly in season, in the wetsuit, with sighting.

### Step 5: Race-specific rehearsal before the event
In the final 6–8 weeks, at least three swims should replicate race conditions: correct distance, wetsuit, open water, and a hard first 200 m to rehearse the start surge and the recovery from it.
