# Cycling Injury & Bike Fit

> **This document is not a substitute for medical or professional fitting advice.** Every symptom below should send the athlete to a doctor, physiotherapist, or qualified bike fitter — not to a self-administered adjustment. What follows describes what is generally known about the relationship between position and symptom so the conversation with a professional is better informed. Never diagnose. Numbness, pain that persists off the bike, and any skin that has broken are professional referrals, not training adjustments.

## Summary
`injury-prevention.md` is a running document — its framework is impact, tissue tolerance, and load progression. Almost none of that governs cycling. Cycling injuries are overwhelmingly **positional and repetitive rather than impact-driven**: a rider turns the cranks roughly 4,500–5,500 times per hour, so a three-hour ride repeats the same joint path around 15,000 times, and a position error of a few millimetres is repeated every one of them. This changes the entire diagnostic logic. In running, the first question is "did the load go up too fast?" In cycling, the first question is "what changed about the position?" — new shoes, a new saddle, a slammed stem, a bike fit, a cleat that crept. Volume matters, but it usually acts as the amplifier of a position problem rather than the cause.

The other distinctive feature is that several cycling complaints are **neural or vascular rather than musculoskeletal** — hand numbness, foot numbness, perineal numbness — and those follow a different rule entirely: they are not to be trained through at any volume.

## Key Concepts

### Why Cycling Injuries Are Different
| | Running | Cycling |
|---|---|---|
| Primary mechanism | Impact, eccentric loading, bone stress | Repetition within a fixed, externally constrained position |
| Usual cause | Load progressed faster than tissue adapted | Position error, or an unchanged position held too long |
| First question | What did volume do? | What changed on the bike? |
| Typical onset | Gradual over weeks of building | Often traceable to a specific change or a specific long ride |
| Bone stress injury | Common and serious | Rare — cycling is non-weight-bearing, which is also a bone-density concern in cycling-only athletes |
| Can be trained around | Often, by cross-training | Often, by fixing the position rather than resting |

The corollary that matters most: **rest often does not fix a cycling injury.** It relieves the symptom, and the symptom returns on the first long ride back, because the geometry that caused it is unchanged.

### The Fit Variables, Roughly in Order of How Often They Cause Problems
| Variable | What it governs | Typical error |
|---|---|---|
| **Saddle height** | Knee extension at the bottom of the stroke, hip rocking | Too high — posterior knee pain, hamstring strain, hip rock, saddle discomfort. Too low — anterior knee pain |
| **Saddle fore/aft (setback)** | Where the knee sits over the pedal through the stroke | Too far forward loads the front of the knee; too far back loads the hamstring and the hands |
| **Cleat fore/aft** | Effective lever from ankle to pedal | Too far forward — calf load, hot foot, Achilles |
| **Cleat rotation and float** | Whether the knee can track its natural path | Locked-in rotation is a classic cause of medial and lateral knee pain |
| **Stance width / Q-factor** | Frontal-plane knee tracking | Too narrow for a wide-hipped rider drives the knee inward |
| **Reach and drop (stem, bar)** | Load through the hands, neck angle, low-back flexion | Too long or too low — neck, low back, hands |
| **Saddle model and tilt** | Soft-tissue pressure, pelvic support | Nose-down shifts weight onto the hands; wrong width leaves the sit bones unsupported |
| **Crank length** | Hip and knee closure at the top of the stroke | Too long for the rider's hip mobility — anterior knee and hip-flexor complaints, worse in an aero position |

### Symptom → Likely Cause → First Question
Read this as a conversation opener with a fitter or clinician, not as a fix.

| Symptom | Common positional associations | Usual first thing checked | Escalate immediately if |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior knee** (front, around/under kneecap) | Saddle too low, saddle too far forward, cranks too long, big-gear low-cadence riding, rapid volume ramp | Saddle height and setback; cadence habits | Swelling, locking, giving way, or pain on stairs off the bike |
| **Posterior knee** (behind the knee) | Saddle too high, cleat too far forward, sudden reach for a bigger gear | Saddle height | Sharp or persistent pain — hamstring or popliteal structures |
| **Lateral knee / IT band** | Stance width, cleat rotation restricting natural tracking, saddle too high | Cleat rotation and float, stance width | Pain that persists walking |
| **Medial knee** | Cleat rotation, knee tracking inward, stance too narrow | Cleat alignment and wedges | Persistent, or with instability |
| **Low back** | Excessive reach or drop, limited hamstring or hip mobility, long time in one position | Reach and drop; time out of the saddle | Radiating pain, numbness, or leg weakness — that is a nerve, not a fit |
| **Neck and upper trapezius** | Drop too great, reach too long, riding with locked elbows, helmet or eyewear forcing extension | Bar height, elbow bend | Persistent headaches or arm symptoms |
| **Hand numbness — little and ring finger** | Ulnar nerve compression at the palm; too much weight on the hands, bars too low or far | Weight distribution, glove padding, bar tape, hand-position changes during a ride | **Any numbness lasting past the ride — stop riding that position and see a professional** |
| **Hand numbness — thumb and index** | Median nerve, carpal tunnel; wrist extension angle | Bar rotation, hood angle, wrist neutrality | As above |
| **Foot numbness / "hot foot"** | Shoes too tight or too narrow, cleat too far forward, high pressure under the ball of the foot | Shoe fit and volume, cleat position | Persistent numbness |
| **Perineal / genital numbness** | Saddle width, tilt, or model; too much weight forward | Saddle choice and tilt — this is not something to tolerate | **Immediately. Any perineal numbness is a stop-and-change signal, full stop** |
| **Saddle sores** | Saddle pressure, chamois fit or age, hygiene, long unbroken time seated | Chamois condition, saddle, standing regularly | Broken skin, spreading redness, fever — infection risk, see a doctor |
| **Achilles / calf** | Cleat too far forward, saddle too high, sudden low-cadence work | Cleat fore/aft, saddle height | Sudden sharp pain — tendon rupture is a medical emergency |

### The Rules That Do Not Bend
1. **Numbness is never a "push through" symptom.** It is nerve or vascular compression. It resolves by removing the compression, and delaying that risks lasting damage.
2. **Pain that persists off the bike is not a fit issue to be tinkered with.** It is a clinical question.
3. **Change one thing at a time**, and give it two to three rides. Changing saddle height and cleat position together makes the outcome uninterpretable.
4. **Saddle height changes are made in millimetres, not centimetres.** 2–3 mm is a meaningful adjustment.
5. **A new symptom after a new component is that component until proven otherwise.** New shoes, new saddle, new pedals, a rebuilt bike, a bike fit — check that first.

### Saddle Height Starting Points
Every method below is a *starting point* for a dynamic assessment, not an answer.

- **Knee angle at the bottom of the stroke:** roughly 25–35° of flexion, measured with a goniometer or from video, with the foot in its natural pedalling position. This is the most widely used reference and the one most fitters work from.
- **LeMond method:** inseam × 0.883 = centre of bottom bracket to top of saddle. A quick first approximation with no account of crank length, shoe stack, or cleat position.
- **Heel-on-pedal:** leg straight with the heel on the pedal at the bottom of the stroke. Crude, and it systematically ignores foot and ankle mechanics.
- **The observation that beats all three:** hips rocking on the saddle means it is too high, regardless of what any formula says.

### Cleat Position
The traditional rule places the ball of the foot over or slightly behind the pedal spindle. The trend across fitting practice has moved the cleat *rearward* from that — a more mid-foot position reduces calf load and plantar-flexion demand, at some cost to sprint and standing-effort power. For a long-distance or triathlon rider the rearward bias is generally favourable; for a criterium sprinter it is not.

Rotational alignment matters more than fore/aft for knee pain. Float allows the knee to find its own path; a cleat that locks the foot at an angle the knee does not want is a common and easily missed cause of medial or lateral knee pain.

### Crank Length
Shorter cranks reduce hip and knee closure at the top of the pedal stroke. That matters for three groups: riders with anterior knee pain, riders with limited hip mobility, and anyone holding an aggressive aero position where hip closure is already severe. The measurable power cost of going shorter is small to nil in the research; the comfort and position benefit can be substantial. Historically cranks were sized to frame size rather than to rider anatomy, which left many shorter riders on cranks that were too long.

### Bone Density — the Cycling-Specific Long-Term Risk
Cycling is non-weight-bearing, and cyclists who train exclusively on the bike show lower bone mineral density than both runners and sedentary controls in multiple studies. This is not an injury that shows up in a training week; it shows up as a fracture in a crash or in later life. The mitigation is resistance training and some impact loading — see `strength-training.md`. For an athlete who has moved to cycling *because* running is no longer tolerated, this is a live consideration and worth naming explicitly rather than leaving implied.

### What Cycling Injuries Are Genuinely Load-Driven
Position is the usual suspect, but not always:
- **Patellar and quadriceps tendinopathy** after a sharp increase in volume, in climbing volume, or in low-cadence work
- **Iliotibial band symptoms** following a large ramp, especially with a position change in the same period
- **Neck and low back** on a multi-day tour, where the cause is unbroken hours in one position rather than the position being wrong
- **Overuse of the hands and perineum on long events** — the mechanism is uninterrupted contact time, and the fix is changing position frequently. See the contact-points section of `multiday-events.md`.

For these, the ramp guidance in `volume-progression.md` applies unchanged — roughly 10% weekly, step back every 3–4 weeks.

### Return to Riding After Injury or Surgery
The bike is the standard rehabilitation modality for lower-limb injury precisely because load is controllable and impact is absent — but "cycling is low-impact" is not the same as "cycling is safe for this knee." A stationary bike at low resistance and high cadence loads the knee very differently from a climb at 60 rpm. Compressive patellofemoral load rises steeply with pedal force, which means **the controllable variables are cadence and gradient, not duration**.

General shape of a return, subject entirely to clinical clearance:
1. Flat or indoor, low resistance, high cadence (85–95 rpm), short duration
2. Extend duration before adding any resistance
3. Add gentle rolling terrain, keeping cadence high
4. Add sustained climbing last, with gearing chosen so cadence never has to drop below about 75 rpm
5. Low-cadence or standing efforts last, if at all

## What the Experts Say

### Andy Pruitt (Boulder Center for Sports Medicine; *Complete Medical Guide for Cyclists*)
- **The reference text for cycling-specific injury.** Frames cycling complaints as the interaction of anatomy and machine, and treats the bike as the adjustable variable when anatomy cannot be.
- **Anatomy varies more than bikes do:** leg-length discrepancy, forefoot varus, and Q-angle differences are common and are accommodated with shims, wedges, and cleat adjustment rather than trained away.
- **Anterior knee pain is the signature cycling injury** and is most often about saddle height, saddle setback, and gear selection.

### Phil Burt (former Head of Physiotherapy, British Cycling and Team Sky; *Bike Fit*)
- **"Fit the bike to the rider, not the rider to the bike"** — and equally, do not chase a position the rider's mobility cannot hold.
- **The best position is the one the rider can hold for the duration of the event.** An aerodynamically superior position that collapses after 90 minutes is slower.
- **Fit is a process, not an event.** Mobility, mileage, goals, and age change; the position should be revisited.
- Skeptical of formula-driven fitting and of the idea that there is one correct position for a given body.

### Steve Hogg (Australian fitting tradition)
- **Function over numbers.** Assesses how a rider actually stabilizes on the bike — where the pressure goes, whether the pelvis is level, whether the rider can hold position without gripping the bars.
- **Cleat and foot correction first.** Argues the foot–pedal interface is the foundation and that fixing knee tracking usually starts below the knee, not at the saddle.

### Protocol-driven fitting systems (Retül, Specialized Body Geometry, and similar)
- **Motion capture and repeatable measurement** make a fit reproducible and comparable over time, which formula and eyeball methods are not.
- The critique from the Burt and Hogg tradition: a target joint-angle range derived from population data is a starting point, and a system that treats it as an endpoint will put some riders in a position that measures correctly and feels wrong.

### The endurance-coaching view (Koop, Friel, Dixon)
- Bike fit is treated as a **prerequisite to training rather than part of it** — solved once before a block begins, then not allowed to consume attention.
- Friel on masters riders: mobility declines with age and positions should become less aggressive accordingly; the common error is defending a position from a decade ago.

## Where They Agree
1. **The foot–pedal interface is the foundation.** Almost every source starts knee complaints at the cleat.
2. **Saddle height and setback explain most knee pain.** Anterior with low or forward, posterior with high.
3. **Numbness is compression and is not negotiable.** No source treats it as something to ride through.
4. **Small changes, one at a time.** Millimetres, and a few rides between changes.
5. **The position must be holdable.** A position the rider cannot maintain for the event duration is the wrong position however good it looks in a fitting studio.
6. **New symptom, new component.** Investigate what changed before investigating the athlete.

## Where They Disagree

### Formula vs Dynamic Assessment
- **Systems view:** measurable joint-angle targets make fit reproducible and teachable.
- **Hogg / Burt:** population ranges are a starting point; how the rider stabilizes and where pressure goes matters more than whether the knee angle reads 30°.
- **Practical position:** use a formula to get in the range, then judge by symptoms, comfort, and whether the rider is stable. Any measured target that produces pain is wrong for that rider.

### Cleat Fore/Aft
- **Traditional:** ball of the foot over the spindle.
- **Mid-foot advocates:** further back reduces calf and Achilles load with negligible power cost for sustained efforts.
- **Resolution by event.** Rearward for long-course, triathlon, and anyone with calf or Achilles history. Traditional for sprinting and standing efforts.

### How Aggressive an Aero Position Is Worth
- **Aerodynamic argument:** frontal area is the dominant cost above about 25 km/h and drop pays for itself.
- **Fit argument:** a position that closes the hip beyond what mobility allows costs power, comfort, and the run in a triathlon.
- **Both are measurable and should be measured together** — sustainable power in the position, not just the drag number.

### Whether Fit or Load Comes First for Knee Pain
- **Fit tradition:** position until proven otherwise.
- **Coaching tradition:** look at the ramp rate, the cadence habits, and the climbing volume first.
- **Honest answer:** ask what changed. A gradual onset during a big build points to load; an onset after a new saddle, new shoes, or a fit points to position.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 1: Rule out the clinical question first
Pain that persists off the bike, any numbness, swelling, locking, giving way, broken skin, or anything worsening across several days is a referral. Say so plainly and stop there. Everything below assumes a symptom that appears on the bike and resolves off it, and that a professional has been or is being consulted.

### Step 2: Ask what changed
In order of likelihood: new shoes or cleats; cleats worn or crept; new saddle or saddle moved; bike serviced or rebuilt; a recent bike fit; a new bike; a sharp increase in volume, climbing, or low-cadence work; new terrain; a position held longer than ever before.

A symptom with no change behind it usually means the position was marginal all along and the volume finally found it.

### Step 3: Locate the symptom and form a hypothesis
Use the symptom table above. Anterior knee → saddle low or forward. Posterior knee → saddle high. Hands → weight distribution. Neck → drop and reach.

### Step 4: Change one variable, in millimetres
Saddle height in 2–3 mm steps. Cleat in 2 mm steps. Give each change two to three rides before judging. Record what was changed and when — a fit log is worth more than any single measurement.

### Step 5: Adjust the training around it
While the fit question is open:
- Cadence up, force down. Higher cadence at lower force is the protective direction for the knee.
- Gearing chosen so climbs can be spun, not ground.
- No low-cadence torque work.
- Shorter rides, more often, rather than one long one — most positional symptoms are time-in-position problems.
- Stand and change hand position regularly; on long rides, deliberately and on a schedule.

### Step 6: Escalate to a professional fitter
If two or three single-variable changes have not resolved it, stop adjusting. The pattern of a rider making repeated changes across weeks is how a single fixable problem becomes three overlapping ones.

### Step 7: Address the non-positional risks
- Resistance training for bone density, particularly for a cycling-dominant athlete. See `strength-training.md`.
- Hip and hamstring mobility, which determines how much reach and drop the rider can hold.
- Ramp guardrails per `volume-progression.md`, which still apply even though position is the more common cause.
