# Triathlon

## Summary
Triathlon is not three sports trained in parallel — it is one sport in which three disciplines compete for the same finite recovery budget. The central problem is allocation, not any individual discipline's training. Every hour swum is an hour not run; every hard bike session raises the cost of the next day's run. The literature agrees on the structural answer (frequency over duration, bike volume as the backbone of long-course racing, run off the bike as the discriminating skill) and disagrees sharply on intensity distribution and on how much the swim is worth. For an experienced athlete the failure mode is almost never undertraining a discipline — it is accumulating three sports' worth of training stress and recovering as though it were one sport's worth.

## Key Concepts

### The Allocation Problem
Total training stress is shared across disciplines, but injury risk is not. Running carries by far the highest injury cost per unit of training stress; swimming the lowest; cycling in between and heavily dependent on fit. This asymmetry drives the standard long-course allocation:

| Discipline | Typical share of weekly hours | Injury cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Swim | 15–20% | Lowest | Shoulder-limited, not systemically costly |
| Bike | 50–60% | Moderate | Cheap aerobic stress; the backbone of long-course fitness |
| Run | 25–30% | Highest | Most fitness per hour *and* most injuries per hour |

The bike is where volume is safe to accumulate. This is why long-course plans are bike-heavy and why athletes who try to build a triathlon engine through running break.

### Race Distances and What Each Rewards
| Distance | Swim | Bike | Run | Dominant limiter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sprint | 750 m | 20 km | 5 km | Threshold tolerance, transitions |
| Olympic | 1.5 km | 40 km | 10 km | Sustained threshold |
| 70.3 | 1.9 km | 90 km | 21.1 km | Bike pacing discipline, fuelling |
| Ironman | 3.8 km | 180 km | 42.2 km | Durability, fuelling, heat, pacing |

The swim is ~10% of an Ironman day and ~17% of a sprint. Investment should scale accordingly, and routinely does not.

### Intensity Factor and Bike Pacing
The most consequential number in long-course racing. Intensity Factor (IF) is average normalized power as a fraction of FTP.

| Race | Target bike IF | Consequence of exceeding it |
|---|---|---|
| Olympic | 0.85–0.90 | Run degrades modestly |
| 70.3 | 0.78–0.85 | Run degrades sharply above ~0.85 |
| Ironman | 0.65–0.75 | Run collapses above ~0.78 |

The rule that matters: **the bike does not have a finish line.** An Ironman bike ridden 5% too hard does not cost 5% of the run — it can cost the run entirely. Nearly every catastrophic marathon split in long-course racing is a bike pacing error, not a run fitness deficit.

Without power, heart rate is a poor substitute on race day (cardiac drift, heat, excitement). RPE plus a hard ceiling on the first 30 minutes is more reliable than a heart-rate cap.

### Brick Workouts
A run immediately following a bike. The specific adaptation is neuromuscular and cardiovascular — the transition from a seated, high-cadence, quad-dominant action to weight-bearing running is a discrete skill.

- **Short brick (10–20 min run off any bike):** frequent, cheap, low cost. Teaches the transition without meaningful added fatigue.
- **Race-simulation brick:** long ride at race IF followed by a run of 20–45 min at race pace. Expensive; a key session, not a weekly one.
- **The consensus:** frequency of short bricks beats occasional heroic ones. The adaptation saturates quickly.

What a brick does *not* do is build run fitness. It builds the transition. Run fitness comes from running.

### Transitions
Free time, and the only part of the sport where preparation returns seconds for zero physiological cost. T1 and T2 combined can differ by 3–5 minutes between a practised and unpractised athlete in a long-course race — more than most athletes gain from a season of swim work.

### Durability / Fatigue Resistance
The trait that separates long-course performers is not peak FTP or VO2max but the ability to still produce power and run economically after 3–5 hours of work. It is now recognized as a distinct, separately trainable quality.

Trained by:
- Long rides with efforts placed *late* in the session, not early
- Runs off the bike, at moderate rather than maximal intensity
- Fasted or low-carb-availability sessions, used sparingly and never before key work

## What the Experts Say

### Joe Friel (The Triathlete's Training Bible)
- **Annual periodization with discipline-specific limiters:** identify which of swim, bike, or run is the limiter for the target race, and allocate the increment of training time there rather than spreading it evenly.
- **Frequency over duration:** for skill-dependent disciplines (especially swim), more frequent short sessions beat fewer long ones.
- **Masters athletes need more recovery, not less intensity.** Friel's later work argues that intensity is the last thing to give up with age; volume and recovery days are the adjustable variables.
- **Build races as training:** shorter races as key sessions within the build, rather than treating every start as a peak.

### Matt Dixon (Purple Patch; Fast-Track Triathlete)
- **Four pillars: endurance, strength, nutrition, recovery.** Recovery is an active pillar, not the absence of training. The most common error among time-limited amateurs is treating recovery as the residual.
- **"Consistency trumps heroics."** The athlete who trains 10 sustainable hours weekly for a year beats the one who alternates 16-hour weeks with breakdowns.
- **Time-limited athletes should cut volume, not intensity.** Explicitly rejects the idea that an amateur with 10 hours should train like a pro with 25 at reduced scale.
- **Sleep and fuelling are training.** Under-fuelled sessions are not free; they are stress without adaptation.

### Jason Koop (CTS)
- **Workload-based, specificity-driven.** Interval prescription matched to the demands of the actual event, not to generic templates.
- **Strength training as injury prevention.** 2×/week during base and build, dropping to maintenance in peak and taper — scheduled on easy days or after hard sessions, never before key work.
- **Skeptical of gym-based muscular endurance** in favour of terrain- and sport-specific loading.

### Stephen Seiler (polarized training)
- **80/20 distribution:** roughly 80% of sessions genuinely easy (below the first ventilatory threshold), 20% genuinely hard. The failure mode is the middle — moderately hard work that accumulates fatigue without the adaptive return of either end.
- Applies across all three disciplines. In triathlon the risk is compounded: three sports' worth of "moderate" adds to a large grey-zone volume even when no single session looks hard.

### Scott Johnston / Uphill Athlete
- Not triathlon-specific, but the **aerobic deficiency syndrome** framework transfers: a large gap between aerobic and anaerobic threshold indicates an underdeveloped aerobic base, and the correction is low-intensity volume — which in triathlon should be loaded onto the bike, where it is cheapest.

## Where They Agree
1. **The bike is the backbone of long-course fitness.** Cheapest aerobic stress per unit of injury risk.
2. **Run off the bike is a distinct, trainable skill** and short frequent bricks build it better than rare long ones.
3. **Bike pacing determines the run.** Over-biking is the single most common cause of a bad long-course race.
4. **Recovery is a pillar, not a gap.** Three sports compete for one recovery budget.
5. **Consistency beats peak weeks.** Sustainable volume held for months outperforms oscillation between overload and breakdown.
6. **Transitions and race logistics are free time** and are systematically under-practised.

## Where They Disagree

### Intensity Distribution
- **Seiler:** strict polarization, 80/20, the middle is wasted stress.
- **Friel:** more tolerant of sustained threshold work, particularly for Olympic and 70.3 where the race itself is near threshold.
- **Dixon:** for time-limited amateurs, argues the opposite of polarization at the margin — with only 8–10 hours, a higher proportion of quality is warranted because there is not enough volume to make easy work productive.
- **Resolution:** the more hours available, the more polarized the distribution should be. At 6–8 hours, Dixon. At 15–20, Seiler.

### How Much Swim Investment Is Justified
- **Swim-focused coaches:** the swim sets up the race; exiting the water in a bad state costs more than the clock shows.
- **Long-course coaches (Dixon, Filliol):** it is 10% of an Ironman and routinely over-trained relative to the bike.
- **Resolution is distance-dependent.** Sprint and Olympic reward swim investment; Ironman rewards arriving fresh far more than arriving fast.

### Long-Run Necessity in Ironman Training
- **Traditional:** progressive long runs to 2:30–3:00.
- **Dixon and others:** the long run is the highest-injury-cost session in the sport, and much of its adaptation can be obtained from long rides plus moderate runs off the bike, at a fraction of the risk.
- **Practical position:** for an athlete with a history of running injury, capping the long run and shifting the durability work onto the bike is defensible and increasingly mainstream.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 1: Identify the limiter for *this* race
Compare each split against age-group norms for the target distance. The limiter is where the athlete is furthest behind, weighted by that discipline's share of race time. A weak swim in an Ironman is usually not worth fixing; a weak bike always is.

### Step 2: Allocate hours
Start at 15–20 / 50–60 / 25–30 (swim / bike / run) for long course. Shift toward the limiter, but never move run above ~35% — that is where injury risk climbs disproportionately.

### Step 3: Set frequency floors before adding duration
- Swim: 3× weekly minimum to hold feel for the water; 4–5 to improve
- Bike: 3–4× weekly, one long
- Run: 3–4× weekly, one long, at least one off the bike

An athlete who cannot meet the frequency floors has too many disciplines for the hours available and should reduce race ambition rather than compress sessions.

### Step 4: Distribute intensity by available volume
- Under 10 h/week: ~70/30 easy/hard, per Dixon
- 10–15 h/week: ~80/20
- Over 15 h/week: 80/20 or more polarized, per Seiler

Count grey-zone work across *all three* sports before concluding the distribution is polarized.

### Step 5: Protect the run
Running is the highest-injury-cost discipline and the one where the race is won or lost. Schedule key runs when fresh, not after hard bike days. Do not stack a long run the day after a long ride unless fatigue resistance is the explicit target of that block.

### Step 6: Rehearse the race, not just the fitness
In the final 6–8 weeks: race-IF bike sessions, fuelling at race rate, transitions practised, wetsuit and open water, and at least one race-simulation brick. The most common late-block error is arriving fit and unrehearsed.

### Step 7: Taper
Volume drops 40–60% over 2 weeks; intensity is retained at reduced volume. See `taper.md`. Triathlon-specific note: reduce swim last — it is the cheapest to maintain and the most sensitive to detraining feel.
