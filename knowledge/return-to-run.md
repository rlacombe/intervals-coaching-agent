# Return to Running

> **Read this before anything else in this file.** Return to running after an
> injury, a surgery, or a long layoff is a clinical question before it is a
> training question. Nothing here substitutes for a doctor, physiotherapist, or
> surgeon. Where imaging is pending or clearance has not been given, there is no
> progression to build — the honest answer is to wait, and a companion that
> softens that is not being kind. Everything below assumes clearance already
> exists and describes what is generally known about how a return is structured
> so the athlete can have a better conversation with the person who gave it.

## Summary
Returning to running is a distinct discipline from training, and it fails in a
characteristic way: not usually because the athlete rushed a known plan, but
because there was no plan and no criteria at all. Two decisions govern it — when
to start, and what permits the next step up — and sports medicine has moved
decisively from time-based protocols ("week three: three minutes running, two
walking") toward **criteria-based** ones, where the athlete advances by meeting a
standard rather than by a calendar arriving. The reason is that tissue heals on
its own schedule and the schedule differs by tissue: bone remodels over months,
tendon adapts over weeks to months and is load-specific, muscle is fastest. The
trap that catches experienced athletes is that **cardiovascular fitness returns
far faster than structural tolerance** — the engine feels ready weeks before the
tissue is, and the fitter the athlete, the wider that gap. A returning
ultrarunner's aerobic system will happily write cheques their tendons cannot
cash.

## Key Concepts

### Criteria-Based Beats Time-Based
A time-based protocol says what to do in week four. A criteria-based one says
what must be true before week four's session is attempted. The second is better
for an obvious reason — two athletes with the same injury are not in the same
place at the same time — and for a less obvious one: a criteria-based plan gives
the athlete a rule to fail against, which is what stops a bad week from becoming
a re-injury.

Use a time-based table as the *shape* of a progression. Use criteria as the
*permission* to move through it.

### Entry Criteria — Before the First Run
Sport-specific standards vary and belong to the treating clinician, but the
common gates across return-to-sport practice are:

- **Clinical clearance**, explicitly for running, not for "activity"
- **Pain-free walking** for at least 30 continuous minutes
- **No swelling** at rest or after a full day of normal activity
- **Full, symmetrical range of motion**
- **Strength within roughly 10% of the uninjured side** on the relevant tests
- **Single-leg loading tolerance** — the ability to load the limb on one leg
  without pain, progressing to hopping where the clinician has cleared it. For
  bone stress injuries in particular, pain-free single-leg hopping is a common
  gate and is not something to self-administer early.

An athlete who cannot walk for thirty minutes without symptoms is not ready to
run for one, whatever their fitness says.

### The 24-Hour Rule
The single most useful rule in the whole process: **symptoms must return to
their pre-session baseline within 24 hours.** Not "must be gone" — must be back
to where they were before the session.

If they have not, the last session was too much. Repeat the previous step rather
than advancing, and if it happens twice, go back a step and involve the
clinician. This rule works because it is objective, it is checkable the next
morning, and it does not require the athlete to judge intensity honestly in the
moment.

### The Pain-Monitoring Question — and Where It Does Not Apply
Modern tendinopathy rehabilitation accepts **low-level pain during loading**
(commonly framed as up to 2 or 3 on a 10-point scale) provided it settles within
24 hours and does not worsen week to week. Loading is the treatment for tendon,
and waiting for pain-free is often waiting forever.

**This does not generalize.** For bone stress injuries the standard is pain-free
— bone does not benefit from being loaded through pain, and the failure mode is
a completed fracture rather than a slower recovery. Cartilage and meniscal
problems, joint effusion, and any post-surgical situation follow whatever the
surgeon says and nothing else.

Getting this distinction wrong in either direction causes harm: pain-free-only
for a tendon stalls the recovery, and pain-tolerant for a bone risks the
fracture. The companion's job is to know which conversation is happening, and to
send the question to the clinician when it is not clear.

### Tissue Timelines, Approximately
These are orientation, not prescription, and every one of them belongs to the
treating professional in a specific case.

| Tissue | Rough timeline | What governs it |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle strain | Days to weeks by grade | Progressive loading; eccentric strength before speed |
| Tendon | Weeks to months | Load-specific adaptation; tendon responds to load, not to rest |
| Bone stress injury | 6–12 weeks minimum before running, often longer | Remodelling; site matters enormously — high-risk sites (femoral neck, anterior tibia, navicular, sacrum) are a different category entirely |
| Cartilage / meniscus | Months, and often permanently altered tolerance | Surgeon's protocol; compressive and shear load, not just volume |
| Post-surgical | Entirely protocol-dependent | The surgeon. There is no general answer |
| Deconditioning only (no injury) | Weeks | Tissue tolerance still lags fitness — the same rules apply |

### The Ordering Rule
Add one variable at a time, in this order. Skipping ahead is the most common
self-coached error.

1. **Frequency** — get to three or four short sessions a week
2. **Duration** — extend the running portion, shrink the walking
3. **Continuity** — move from walk/run intervals to continuous running
4. **Terrain** — flat and forgiving first, then rolling
5. **Uphill** — before downhill, always
6. **Downhill** — the eccentric load is the highest-risk element in running.
   See `downhill-training.md`
7. **Speed** — strides before intervals, intervals before racing
8. **Consecutive days** — one of the last things to add, not one of the first
9. **Long runs** — last. See `long-runs.md`

Note where speed sits. Runners returning from injury reliably want speed back
early because it is the most obvious marker of being "back," and it is one of
the last things that should be added.

### A Walk/Run Progression Shape
A conventional starting shape for an uncomplicated return, with the 24-hour rule
governing every advancement and each step held for at least two or three
sessions before moving on:

| Step | Session | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 min run / 4 min walk × 5 | 3×/week |
| 2 | 1 min run / 3 min walk × 6 | 3×/week |
| 3 | 2 min run / 3 min walk × 6 | 3×/week |
| 4 | 3 min run / 2 min walk × 6 | 3×/week |
| 5 | 4 min run / 1 min walk × 6 | 3–4×/week |
| 6 | 5 min run / 1 min walk × 5 | 3–4×/week |
| 7 | 10 min continuous, ×2 with a walk between | 3–4×/week |
| 8 | 20 min continuous | 3–4×/week |
| 9 | 30 min continuous | 4×/week |
| 10 | Begin adding terrain, then hills, then strides | — |

Total running time — not distance — is the metric that matters here. Distance
invites pace comparison, and pace comparison is how a returning runner talks
themselves into a bad session.

Progression from step 9 onward follows normal volume rules: roughly 10% weekly,
step-back every third or fourth week. See `volume-progression.md`.

### Walking Is the Load, Not the Filler
The walk intervals are not waiting. They are what makes the running intervals
survivable, and cutting them short is the most common way an athlete quietly
doubles their load. An athlete who has been told "1 minute run, 4 minutes walk"
and does 1 and 2 has not made a small adjustment — they have gone from 20% of
the session running to 33%.

### Cadence as a Load Lever
Increasing step rate by around 5–10% reduces per-step impact and, notably,
reduces load at the knee, because it shortens the stride and moves the foot
strike closer to under the body. It is one of the few running-form
interventions with reasonable supporting evidence, and it is directly relevant
to a knee-limited return. It is not free — it shifts some load toward the calf
and Achilles — so introduce it gradually and not simultaneously with a volume
step.

### The Cross-Training Bridge, and Its Limit
Cycling, pool running, and the elliptical maintain aerobic fitness while the
structure adapts, and for an athlete returning from a long layoff they make the
return far more pleasant. Pool running is the most specific of the three,
reproducing the running motion without impact.

The limit worth stating plainly: **aerobic fitness is not the limiter in a
return to running, so maintaining it does not accelerate the return.** An
athlete with a large cycling base is not further along the return-to-run
progression than one without — they simply have less to rebuild afterwards, and
a greater temptation to skip steps. Cross-training buys the ability to come back
fitter. It does not buy the ability to come back sooner.

### What Load Metrics Are Useless Here
Fitness and fatigue scores (CTL/ATL/TSB) are the wrong instrument for a
returning runner. A 15-minute walk/run generates almost no training load, so the
numbers will say the athlete is detraining and under-recovered while their
tissues are being loaded harder than at any point in months. Track **running
minutes, sessions per week, and symptom response**. Come back to the load model
when continuous running is established.

### Red Flags That Stop the Progression
Straight to the clinician, no adjustment attempted:

- Pain that worsens during a session rather than easing after the first minutes
- Pain that has not returned to baseline within 24 hours, twice
- Any night pain
- Swelling, locking, catching, or the joint giving way
- A limp — if the gait is altered, the session is over
- Focal, pinpoint bone pain, especially if it hurts to press on
- Any new symptom in a different location, which usually means compensation

## What the Experts Say

### Dr. Megan Roche & David Roche (SWAP Running)
- **The clinical voice in the running-coaching space.** Dr. Megan Roche's
  perspective is a medical one — overuse injury, bone health, energy
  availability, and the hormonal contributors that a purely load-based model
  misses.
- **The rope analogy:** tissue tolerance is a rope losing strands. A strand or
  two is survivable; continuing to pull is how the whole thing goes. Applied to
  a return, the point is that the athlete's felt sense of "it's a bit sore but
  fine" is exactly the state in which strands are lost.
- **Consistency is the goal, not any individual run.** A return is judged over
  months, and the athlete who runs three times a week for twelve weeks beats the
  one who runs five times a week for five and then stops.
- **Energy availability matters most during a return**, when appetite often
  drops with training volume while healing demand rises. Under-fuelling a
  recovery is common and directly impairs bone and tendon healing. See
  `nutrition.md`.

### Jason Koop (CTS)
- **Workload framing:** the return is a load progression like any other, and the
  same specificity logic applies — build toward the demands of the target, not
  toward generic mileage.
- **Skeptical of elaborate protocols** in favour of consistent, progressive
  loading and honest assessment of response.

### Scott Johnston / Uphill Athlete
- **The aerobic base can be held elsewhere.** Cycling and uphill hiking maintain
  the aerobic system while impact tolerance rebuilds, which is why an injured
  mountain athlete need not lose a season.
- **Structural tolerance is built by the specific activity.** No amount of
  aerobic work substitutes for the running itself — the tissue adapts to what it
  is asked to do.

### Steve Magness (Science of Running)
- **The lag between systems is the whole problem.** Cardiovascular adaptation
  outpaces musculoskeletal adaptation, and the more trained the athlete, the
  larger the mismatch. The subjective feeling of readiness is generated by the
  faster-adapting system.
- **Gait changes under fatigue** are where late-session injuries come from,
  which argues for ending returning sessions early and fresh.

### The sports-medicine and physiotherapy tradition
- **Criteria-based return to sport** is the mainstream position across
  disciplines, borrowed into running from the ACL and hamstring literature —
  objective strength, function, and symptom thresholds rather than elapsed time.
- **Load is medicine for tendon.** Progressive loading, not rest, is the
  treatment; the pain-monitoring model (low pain acceptable, settling within
  24 hours) comes from this body of work.
- **Bone is the exception**, and high-risk stress-fracture sites are managed
  conservatively and separately.

## Where They Agree
1. **Clearance and criteria before calendars.** Nobody advocates a purely
   time-based return anymore.
2. **The 24-hour symptom response is the governing feedback signal.**
3. **Frequency and duration before intensity and terrain**, with downhill and
   speed late.
4. **Aerobic fitness is not the limiter** and cross-training does not shorten
   the timeline.
5. **Consistency over any single session.** The return is measured in months.
6. **A limp ends the session**, without exception.

## Where They Disagree

### Walk/Run Intervals vs Short Continuous Runs
- **Walk/run tradition:** intermittent loading with recovery between, easier to
  titrate, lower peak accumulated load.
- **Short-continuous advocates:** five minutes of continuous easy running is
  more specific than five one-minute bouts, and the walk intervals disrupt gait
  rhythm without reducing total impact much.
- **Practical resolution:** walk/run for the first weeks and after significant
  injury, where the ability to stop at a defined point matters. Short continuous
  runs are reasonable for a return from layoff or a minor issue in an
  experienced runner.

### How Much Pain Is Acceptable
- **Tendinopathy rehabilitation:** low pain during loading is acceptable and
  probably necessary.
- **Conservative and post-surgical practice:** pain-free, and pain is
  information not noise.
- **Resolution is by tissue, not by philosophy.** Tendon tolerates monitored
  pain. Bone, cartilage, and post-surgical joints do not. When the tissue is
  uncertain, default to pain-free and ask.

### Whether to Maintain Full Cross-Training Volume
- **Fitness-maintenance view:** replace the running load on the bike or in the
  pool so the athlete returns with their base intact.
- **Total-load view:** the reduced systemic load is part of what allows healing,
  and an athlete cross-training at full volume is recovering less than they
  think.
- **Middle position, and the common practice:** maintain meaningful aerobic work
  but not a full replacement of the missing load, and watch total stress
  including the rehabilitation work itself.

### How Fast to Progress Once Continuous Running Is Established
- **Conservative:** stay under the 10% rule for months; the tissue is still
  behind the fitness.
- **Koop and the workload tradition:** past the fragile early phase, a returning
  athlete with a long history rebuilds faster than a novice and holding them to
  novice rates wastes the season.
- **Both accept the same brake:** symptom response governs, and the disagreement
  only matters for an athlete who has none.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 0: Is this a training question yet?
If imaging is pending, clearance has not been given, or the diagnosis is
unknown, **there is no progression to build.** Say so directly. Offer to plan
the cross-training that maintains fitness in the meantime, and offer to have the
progression ready for the day clearance arrives. Do not build a conditional
running plan "for when the results come back" — it becomes the plan.

### Step 1: Confirm the entry criteria
Walk 30 minutes symptom-free, no swelling, symmetrical range of motion and
strength, single-leg loading tolerated, and clinician clearance specifically for
running. Any gap sends it back to the clinician.

### Step 2: Establish the baseline and the rule
Agree the symptom baseline explicitly — what the athlete feels on a normal day —
because the 24-hour rule is meaningless without it. Agree that a limp ends a
session and that two consecutive 24-hour failures means going back a step and
making a phone call.

### Step 3: Start at a step that looks too easy
The first session should feel absurd. That is correct and worth naming in
advance, because an experienced athlete will otherwise interpret it as an
insult. The first run is a tissue test, not training.

### Step 4: Hold each step for two or three sessions
Advance only when the step has been completed at least twice with a clean
24-hour response.

### Step 5: Add complexity in the stated order
Frequency, duration, continuity, terrain, uphill, downhill, speed, consecutive
days, long runs. One at a time. Never a volume step and a new variable in the
same week.

### Step 6: Keep the rest of the load honest
Strength work continues throughout — it is the part of the return that most
directly reduces re-injury risk, and it is what most athletes drop the moment
running resumes. See `strength-training.md`. Watch total load across all
disciplines: an athlete returning to running while maintaining a full cycling
week is progressing two things at once. See `volume-progression.md`.

### Step 7: Recognize what "returned" means
The return is complete when the athlete is running their target frequency and
duration on target terrain with a stable symptom baseline — not when they run
their first long run, and not when they hit an old pace. Set that expectation
early, because the first fast run feeling good is the single most dangerous
moment in the whole process.
