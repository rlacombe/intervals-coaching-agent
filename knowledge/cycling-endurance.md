# Cycling Endurance

## Summary
Cycling is the endurance discipline with the best-developed measurement culture, because power is directly observable in a way that running and swimming output is not. That makes training prescription unusually precise and also unusually easy to over-formalize. The core physiology is the same as every other endurance sport — an aerobic base built with volume, a threshold raised with sustained work, and a top end maintained with short hard efforts. What is distinctive is that cycling accumulates aerobic stress at a very low injury cost, which is why it is the backbone of long-course triathlon and the natural home for volume in any athlete with a load-sensitive joint. The main disagreements are about intensity distribution (Seiler's polarization versus sweet-spot advocates) and about how much of a rider's ceiling is FTP versus durability.

## Key Concepts

### Functional Threshold Power (FTP)
The highest power sustainable in a quasi-steady state for roughly an hour. The reference point for everything else.

**Testing options:**
| Protocol | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 20-minute test | Best 20-min power × 0.95 | Most common; requires a good warmup including an opener effort |
| Ramp test | Best 1-min power × 0.75 | Shorter, less painful, tends to overestimate for diesel riders and underestimate for punchy ones |
| 2 × 8 minutes | Average of two 8-min efforts × 0.90 | Kolie Moore / shorter-format variants |
| Full 60 minutes | Actual hour power | Truest, rarely done |

Retest every 6–10 weeks in a build phase. Note that FTP estimated from a ramp test and from a 20-minute test are frequently 5–10 W apart for the same rider — compare like with like across a season.

### Power Zones (Coggan)
| Zone | Name | % FTP | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Active recovery | <55% | Recovery spins |
| 2 | Endurance | 56–75% | Aerobic base — the bulk of volume |
| 3 | Tempo | 76–90% | Aerobic development; the "grey zone" if overused |
| 4 | Threshold | 91–105% | Raises FTP |
| 5 | VO2max | 106–120% | 3–8 min intervals; aerobic ceiling |
| 6 | Anaerobic | 121–150% | 30 s–3 min |
| 7 | Neuromuscular | >150% | Sprints, <30 s |

**Sweet spot** is 88–94% of FTP — the upper end of tempo into the bottom of threshold. It sits deliberately between zones and is the most contested prescription in cycling.

### Durability / Fatigue Resistance
The ability to still produce power after several hours of work. Increasingly recognized as a *separate* trait from FTP, and the one that actually predicts long-course and multi-day performance.

Two riders with identical fresh FTP can differ by 15–20% in their FTP after 3,000 kJ of work. That gap is trainable and is not visible in any fresh test.

**Trained by:** placing efforts *late* in long rides rather than early, long rides at genuine endurance intensity, and — used sparingly — sessions with reduced carbohydrate availability.

**Measured by:** comparing power at a fixed effort early versus late in a long ride, or by tracking power-duration curve after a set kJ of work. Intervals.icu exposes this.

### Cadence
- **Self-selected cadence is usually close to optimal** for trained riders. Deliberate cadence manipulation has a weaker evidence base than cycling folklore suggests.
- **Metabolic cost rises at high cadence; muscular and joint load rise at low cadence.** The tradeoff matters most for riders with knee issues, where higher cadence and lower force is the protective direction.
- **Low-cadence, high-torque work** (50–60 rpm at threshold) is a common strength-endurance prescription. Evidence for it as a distinct adaptation is thin, and it is contraindicated for anyone with patellofemoral or meniscal problems.

### Climbing
- **Sustained climbing is threshold work with a fixed duration** determined by the hill. Power targets should be set by the climb length: a 10-minute climb is a VO2max-adjacent effort, a 60-minute climb is threshold at best.
- **Gearing is a training variable, not just equipment.** A rider who cannot spin a climb at 75+ rpm is doing low-cadence strength work whether they intend to or not.
- **W/kg governs climbing; absolute watts govern the flat.** A heavier rider is not necessarily worse on the bike, only on the gradient.

### Indoor vs Outdoor
- Indoor power is typically 5–10 W lower at the same perceived effort, mostly from heat accumulation. Not a fitness difference.
- Indoor is more time-efficient for structured intervals: no junctions, no descents, no coasting. A 60-minute indoor session can carry more usable stress than 90 minutes outdoors.
- Outdoor is necessary for handling, pacing skill, and — for anyone racing outside — heat and terrain specificity.

### Training Stress and the Ramp Rate
Cycling accumulates training stress fast because it is non-impact and can be sustained for hours. The usual constraint is not tissue tolerance but recovery and life.

Standard guardrails apply: increase weekly load by no more than ~10%, step back every 3–4 weeks, and watch the ratio of a single big day to chronic load. A ride generating 3× the athlete's current fitness score in one day is an outlier, not a training session, and should be treated as a race for recovery purposes.

## What the Experts Say

### Hunter Allen & Andrew Coggan (Training and Racing with a Power Meter)
- **Established the power-zone framework** now standard across the sport, plus Normalized Power, Intensity Factor, and TSS.
- **Normalized Power over average power:** for variable efforts, NP better reflects physiological cost. Average power understates the cost of a surge-heavy ride.
- **Power profiling:** a rider's 5 s / 1 min / 5 min / 20 min power curve characterizes their type (sprinter, pursuiter, climber, time-triallist) and should shape training emphasis.
- **Quadrant analysis:** distinguishes efforts by force and velocity, so two rides with identical TSS can be shown to place very different neuromuscular demands.

### Stephen Seiler (polarized training)
- **80/20:** roughly 80% of sessions below the first ventilatory threshold, 20% genuinely hard, very little in between.
- **The grey zone is the failure mode:** tempo and sweet-spot work is hard enough to accumulate fatigue but not hard enough to drive top-end adaptation, and it displaces the easy volume that builds the base.
- Evidence base is strongest in elite and well-trained endurance athletes across sports.

### Sweet-spot advocates (Frank Overton / FasCat, and the time-crunched tradition)
- **Sweet spot (88–94% FTP) is the most time-efficient FTP driver.** For an athlete with 6–8 hours weekly, there is not enough volume for polarized training to work as intended.
- **Explicit rebuttal of Seiler for amateurs:** 80/20 presumes the 80 is a large absolute number of hours. At low volume, 80% easy is simply not much stimulus.

### Chris Carmichael / CTS (The Time-Crunched Cyclist)
- **High-intensity, low-volume blocks** can produce race-ready fitness in 6–8 weeks for athletes with under 8 hours weekly.
- **Explicitly acknowledges the tradeoff:** this approach builds a fitness that peaks fast and fades fast, and does not build the deep aerobic base that supports long events. Appropriate for criteriums; not for an Ironman or a multi-day tour.

### Joe Friel
- **Periodized annual plan** with base, build, peak, and transition; aerobic base first, intensity layered later.
- **Masters riders:** intensity is the last thing to remove with age; volume and recovery are the adjustable variables.

## Where They Agree
1. **Aerobic base comes from volume at genuinely easy intensity.** All sources agree on the mechanism; they differ on whether an amateur has enough hours to exploit it.
2. **Power is the superior training metric** where available; heart rate lags, drifts with heat and fatigue, and is unusable for short efforts.
3. **FTP alone does not describe a rider.** The power-duration curve, and increasingly durability, matter as much.
4. **Cycling is the cheapest place to put aerobic volume** for any athlete whose running or impact tolerance is limited.
5. **Recovery governs how much of any of this is usable.** See `recovery-overtraining.md`.

## Where They Disagree

### Polarized vs Sweet Spot
- **Seiler:** the middle is wasted; go easy or go hard.
- **FasCat / Carmichael:** at 6–8 hours weekly the middle is the only place with enough stimulus density to drive FTP.
- **Resolution by volume.** Below ~8 h/week, sweet spot is defensible and probably correct. Above ~12 h/week, polarized. In between, mixed. The genuine error in both camps is doing polarized *volume* with grey-zone *intensity* — 12 easy hours that are actually tempo.

### Low-Cadence Strength Work
- **Traditional:** big-gear, low-cadence intervals build "strength on the bike."
- **Evidence-led view:** cycling forces are far below what limits muscular strength, so the adaptation is likely neuromuscular at best. Meanwhile the joint loading is real.
- **For a rider with knee history there is no debate:** spin, do not grind.

### How Much Indoor Is Acceptable
- **Time-efficiency camp:** indoor is strictly better for structured work.
- **Skill camp:** handling, pacing, cornering, and group riding are trainable only outdoors and decay without practice.
- Both are right; the split should follow the demands of the target event.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 1: Establish the reference points
- FTP, tested consistently with one protocol
- Power-duration curve — where is this rider strong and weak?
- Durability: compare normalized power for the last hour of a long ride against the first, at matched perceived effort

### Step 2: Choose the distribution from the hours available
| Weekly hours | Distribution | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| < 6 | Sweet spot / high intensity | Insufficient volume for base-building to work |
| 6–10 | ~70/30, some sweet spot | Transitional |
| 10–15 | 80/20 polarized | Enough easy volume to be productive |
| > 15 | 80/20 or more polarized | Classic endurance model |

### Step 3: Match the work to the event
- **Long course / touring / multi-day:** durability first. Long rides with late efforts, fuelling rehearsal, back-to-back days.
- **Criterium / short course:** repeatability of hard efforts, sprint, anaerobic capacity.
- **Time trial / triathlon bike leg:** sustained threshold and position-specific work — power held in the aero position is the only power that counts.
- **Climbing events:** sustained efforts at the duration of the target climbs, at W/kg.

### Step 4: Set the guardrails
- Weekly load increase ≤ ~10%
- Step-back week every 3–4 weeks at 60–70% volume
- Treat any single ride generating more than ~2× current fitness score as a race, with race-equivalent recovery
- For a rider with knee history: cadence floor on climbs, gearing chosen to permit it, and no low-cadence strength work

### Step 5: Watch the grey zone
Audit actual time-in-zone rather than intended time-in-zone. The most common finding in an athlete who has plateaued is that the easy rides are not easy.
