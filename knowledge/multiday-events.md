# Multi-Day Events

## Summary
A three-day event is not three copies of a one-day event. The physiology that governs a single long day — pacing, fuelling, thermoregulation — is well covered elsewhere; what makes consecutive days distinct is that recovery becomes an in-race variable rather than something that happens afterwards. Overnight glycogen restoration, sleep quality, and contact-point tissue tolerance all become performance limiters, and none of them are trained by doing single long days. The literature here is thinner and more practitioner-derived than for single-day racing — most of it comes from randonneuring, bikepacking, stage racing, and multi-day hiking rather than from sports science. The consistent finding across all of those traditions is that day one is the day athletes get wrong, and day three is where they pay for it.

## Key Concepts

### Supported vs Self-Supported
This distinction changes almost everything and should be established before any other planning.

| | Supported (van, hotels, crew) | Self-supported (bikepacking, carrying) |
|---|---|---|
| Load | Minimal — day kit only | 8–20 kg, changes power requirement and handling |
| Resupply | Solved | A pacing and route constraint |
| Sleep | Bed, shower, controlled temperature | Variable, often poor |
| Overnight fuelling | Full kitchen or restaurant access | Whatever was carried |
| Failure mode | Fatigue, contact points | All of the above plus logistics |

A supported multi-day event is fundamentally a **repeatability** problem: can the athlete produce a similar day three times? A self-supported one adds load-carriage, sleep debt, and logistics on top. Advice written for one does not transfer cleanly to the other.

### Overnight Glycogen Restoration
The central physiological constraint of consecutive days.

- Muscle glycogen resynthesis runs at roughly **5–7% per hour** under good conditions, so full restoration from a heavily depleted state takes **20–24 hours** — longer than the gap between finishing day one and starting day two.
- Consecutive big days therefore require **8–10 g of carbohydrate per kg of body mass per day**, front-loaded into the post-ride window and the evening meal.
- For a 66 kg athlete that is **530–660 g of carbohydrate daily** — roughly 2,100–2,600 kcal from carbohydrate alone, on top of everything else. This is a genuinely hard eating task and it is the most commonly underestimated part of multi-day preparation.
- The first 30–60 minutes after finishing is disproportionately valuable: glycogen synthase activity is elevated and gastric tolerance is usually still good.
- **Practical implication:** the athlete who finishes day one and delays eating for two hours has already compromised day three.

### The Day-One Trap
The single most reliable finding across stage racing, randonneuring, and multi-day hiking.

Athletes arrive fresh, the weather is good, morale is high, and day one is ridden or run 10–15% harder than sustainable. The cost does not appear on day one — it appears on day three as a disproportionate collapse. The correct target for day one is an effort that feels conspicuously easy, roughly one intensity zone below what feels natural.

A useful heuristic: **day one should feel like the easiest day, and finish with the athlete believing they undercooked it.**

### Contact Points
Over multiple days, tissue tolerance at contact points frequently becomes the binding constraint before fitness does. This is under-appreciated because it never limits a single-day effort.

**Cycling:**
- *Saddle/perineum* — the most common cause of abandoning a multi-day tour. Chamois cream, a second pair of shorts, and out-of-saddle breaks every 20–30 minutes. Any existing saddle sore will get worse, not better.
- *Hands* — ulnar and median nerve compression from sustained bar pressure produces numbness and weakness that accumulates across days. Change hand position frequently; padded gloves and bar tape help.
- *Feet* — sustained pedal pressure over many hours. Stiff soles distribute load; cleat position and shoe volume matter far more on day three than day one. Feet swell over consecutive days, so shoes that fit on day one may not on day three.

**Running / hiking:** blisters, toenails, and metatarsal load follow the same pattern — a minor day-one irritation becomes a day-three abandonment.

The general rule: **treat any hot spot on day one as an emergency**, not an annoyance. It will not resolve while the loading continues.

### Sleep as a Performance Variable
- Sleep between days is the highest-leverage recovery tool available and the one most often sacrificed to logistics, socializing, or late finishes.
- A late finish compounds: less time to eat, less time to sleep, worse recovery, slower next day, later finish again.
- Supported events should plan the *finish time* backwards from a target bedtime, not forwards from a start time.

### Pacing Across Days
- Target a roughly **flat or slightly declining** effort profile across days, in absolute terms, which usually means a *rising* perceived effort for the same output.
- Expect a genuine physiological decrement: even well-fuelled and well-slept, sustainable power or pace typically falls a few percent per consecutive day.
- Plan the hardest terrain for day one or two where possible, not day three.

### Training for Multi-Day
The specific adaptation comes from **back-to-back days**, not from single long days.

Progression:
1. Two consecutive moderate days
2. Two consecutive days, one long and one moderate
3. Two consecutive long days
4. Three consecutive days, the third shorter
5. Three consecutive days at target duration — done once, well before the event, not as a final sharpener

Back-to-back weekends are the standard vehicle. This is a well-established ultrarunning practice (see `long-runs.md`) that transfers directly.

The other trainable element is **the overnight turnaround itself** — eating enough, sleeping enough, and starting the second day with the residual fatigue. Simulating this is the point of the back-to-back; doing two long days a week apart trains something else entirely.

## What the Experts Say

### Scott Johnston / Uphill Athlete
- **Back-to-back long days** as the primary tool for building fatigue resistance and time-on-feet durability — the adaptation targets the ability to perform in an already-fatigued state.
- **Volume progression discipline:** no more than ~10%/week, step-back weeks every 3–4. A multi-day event is a large single-week spike and should be planned for accordingly.
- **Time-on-feet, not pace,** as the organizing metric for long efforts.

### Jason Koop (CTS)
- **Recovery hierarchy:** sleep, then energy balance, then time between sessions. In a multi-day event all three are compressed simultaneously, which is precisely why it is harder than the sum of its days.
- **Gut training is trainable:** carbohydrate absorption rates improve with practice. An athlete who has never eaten 500+ g of carbohydrate in a day should not first attempt it during the event.
- **Strong warning on NSAIDs** — elevated risk of hyponatremia and acute kidney injury in endurance settings, compounded over consecutive days of dehydration. This matters more in multi-day events than anywhere else, and they are more tempting there than anywhere else.

### Randonneuring / audax tradition
- **"Eat before you are hungry, drink before you are thirsty, rest before you are tired."** The governing maxim of long-distance cycling, and it is specifically about pre-empting deficits that cannot be recovered from mid-event.
- **Steady pace, minimal stops, early nights.** Time is lost at controls and hotels, not on the road — but sleep is the one place not to economize on a multi-day.
- **Kit conservatism:** nothing new on the event. This applies to shorts, saddle, shoes, and food, not just bikes.

### The Roches (SWAP Running)
- **Total stress capacity as a finite resource** (the pizza metaphor): a multi-day event consumes a very large share, and life stress in the surrounding weeks draws from the same pool.
- **"Overtraining is 1,000 times worse than undertraining."** Arriving slightly undertrained at a multi-day is recoverable; arriving fatigued is not.

### Steve Magness
- **Recovery times by fatigue type:** low-intensity work over 2 hours needs 24–48 hours for glycogen restoration. Consecutive days by definition do not provide that, which is why the fuelling target is elevated rather than normal.
- **Stress competition model:** travel, poor sleep, unfamiliar food, and social demands all draw on the same recovery capacity as the riding.

## Where They Agree
1. **Day one is the day people get wrong.** Universal across every tradition.
2. **Overnight fuelling is the central physiological constraint,** and it requires deliberate over-eating of carbohydrate rather than eating to appetite.
3. **Sleep is the highest-leverage recovery lever** and the most commonly sacrificed.
4. **Back-to-back training days are the specific preparation.** Single long days do not substitute.
5. **Contact points end more multi-day events than fitness does.**
6. **Nothing new on event day** — kit, food, or otherwise.

## Where They Disagree

### How Hard to Train Beforehand
- **Conservative view:** the event is the hard training; arrive fresh and slightly under-prepared rather than fatigued.
- **Specificity view:** three consecutive target-duration days must be rehearsed at least once, or the athlete does not know whether the fuelling and contact-point plan works.
- **Practical resolution:** rehearse at reduced duration (say 60–70%) rather than full, and do it 4–6 weeks out with a genuine recovery block after.

### Fasted / Low-Carb Training for Fat Adaptation
- **Advocates:** improves fat oxidation and reduces reliance on exogenous carbohydrate over long days.
- **Critics (Koop, most sports-nutrition consensus):** performance at intensity suffers, recovery is compromised, and multi-day events are precisely where the carbohydrate deficit compounds.
- **Where the balance sits:** occasional low-availability sessions in base may have a place; carbohydrate restriction anywhere near a multi-day event does not.

### Rest Day Placement Around the Event
- Some traditions advocate a full rest day before day one; others a short opener ride to prime the legs.
- No strong evidence either way. An easy 30–45 minutes with a few short efforts the day before is the common compromise.

## Coaching Decision Tree

### Step 1: Classify the event
Supported or self-supported? Daily distance, elevation, and expected hours? Terrain distribution across days? These determine everything downstream.

### Step 2: Assess repeatability, not capability
The question is not "can the athlete do one day" but "can they do it three times." Look for:
- Any prior back-to-back long days in the training history
- Current chronic training load relative to the daily demand — a single day generating several times the athlete's chronic load is an outlier, and three of them is a very large spike
- Whether the biggest day in the last 12 months is at, above, or below the target daily volume

### Step 3: Build the back-to-back progression
Work up over 8–12 weeks: two moderate days → long plus moderate → two long → three days with a short third. Recover properly after each. Do not stack the progression on top of an already-rising volume trend.

### Step 4: Rehearse the fuelling
Calculate the daily carbohydrate target (8–10 g/kg). Practise hitting it on back-to-back training days, including the post-ride window. This is a trainable skill and it is where most multi-day plans quietly fail.

### Step 5: Solve contact points in training
Every hour on the bike or on foot in training is a test of the same kit that will be used on the event. Any hot spot that appears in a 5-hour training day will appear sooner and worse on day two of the event. Fix it before, not during.

### Step 6: Plan day one deliberately slow
Set an explicit effort ceiling for day one — a power cap, a heart-rate cap, or a rule about staying seated on climbs. Agree it in advance, with any riding partners, because group dynamics are what break day-one pacing.

### Step 7: Plan the evenings
Target finish time, target eating window, target bedtime. Work backwards. On a supported event this is largely a logistics problem and is the highest-return planning anyone does.

### Step 8: Watch for the escalating signs
Across days, the meaningful signals are rising resting heart rate, falling appetite, worsening sleep despite fatigue, and disproportionate effort for the same output. Two or more of these on the morning of the final day warrants shortening it. See `recovery-overtraining.md`.
